SPATIOTEMPORAL HOTSPOT MINING AND SEVERITY PREDICTION OF ROAD ACCIDENTS
USING APACHE SEDONA AND SPARK MLLIB

PHASE 9.5 — RESULTS, DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS

1. PROJECT OVERVIEW
-------------------
This project analyses road traffic collisions in Great Britain using
distributed data processing, spatial analysis, temporal summaries,
machine learning and association-rule mining.

The dataset contains 513,801 collision records from 2021–2025.
The work uses Apache Spark and Apache Sedona for data processing and
spatial operations, alongside Python libraries for analysis and plots.

2. DATA PREPARATION AND SPATIAL COVERAGE
----------------------------------------
The original dataset contains 513,801 records. Of these, 513,748 records
have valid coordinate pairs for spatial processing.

The Great Britain boundary filter retained 513,252 records and identified
496 records outside the boundary. A subsequent spatial join matched
513,250 records to a historic local-authority boundary; 498 records were
unmatched in that join. These counts describe different processing stages
and should not be treated as interchangeable.

The spatial join supports local-authority summaries. It does not represent
road-segment analysis because no road-network segmentation was performed.

3. TEMPORAL RESULTS
-------------------
The original dataset contains the following annual collision counts:

2021: 101,087
2022: 106,004
2023: 104,258
2024: 100,927
2025: 101,525

The highest annual count in this period was in 2022. Counts decreased in
2023 and 2024, then rose slightly in 2025. These are observed dataset
counts, not exposure-adjusted road-safety rates. Differences in traffic
volume, travel distance, reporting and data completeness are not controlled
for in this simple comparison.

4. SPATIAL GRID AND HIGH-COUNT SCREENING
----------------------------------------
A 5 km grid was used as the baseline spatial aggregation unit. The saved
all-years grid contains 7,615 occupied cells.

The Phase 5 year-specific grid output contains 30,552 grid-year records
and sums to 513,252 collision records. A high-count screening threshold
of 64 was used, based on the 95th percentile of grid-year counts.
The screening output contains 1,566 high-count grid-year records.

These cells identify locations with comparatively high observed counts
under the selected grid size and threshold. They are screening candidates,
not statistically confirmed hotspots. Counts can be influenced by cell
size, population, road density, traffic exposure and urban concentration.

5. TEMPORAL HOTSPOT CATEGORIES
------------------------------
The saved temporal classification contains 7,615 occupied grid cells:

Never high-count: 7,229
Persistent:         257
Variable:            68
Emerging:            34
Declining:           27

Persistent cells repeatedly met the selected high-count screening rule
across the analysed years. Emerging and declining categories describe
changes under that rule; they do not establish the causes of those changes.
These labels should be interpreted in relation to the selected grid size,
threshold and study period.

Sensitivity outputs were also generated for 2 km and 10 km grids. Grid
resolution can change cell counts and hotspot patterns, so conclusions
should not be assumed to remain identical across resolutions.

6. SEVERITY CLASSIFICATION
--------------------------
Severity was encoded as:
0 = Fatal
1 = Serious
2 = Slight

The model used a time-based split: 2021–2024 for training
(412,276 records) and 2025 for testing (101,525 records).
The feature matrix contained 134 features.

Baseline Logistic Regression:
Accuracy: 0.7376
Macro F1: 0.2841
Weighted F1: 0.6270

Class-weighted Logistic Regression:
Accuracy: 0.4670
Macro F1: 0.3277
Weighted F1: 0.5365

The class-weighted model improved macro F1 from 0.2841 to 0.3277,
a relative increase of approximately 15.3%, but its overall accuracy
decreased. This illustrates the trade-off between overall accuracy and
performance across imbalanced classes.

The baseline model performed poorly on minority severity classes.
Class weighting improved fatal-class recall to approximately 0.6146,
but fatal-class precision was approximately 0.0350. Therefore, many
predictions labelled fatal were incorrect. This model should not be used
as a standalone operational road-safety decision system.

The 2025 time-based test is useful for evaluating performance on a later
year, but changes in reporting practices, coding and data distributions
may affect the results.

7. ASSOCIATION-RULE MINING
--------------------------
FP-Growth was applied to 513,801 transactions containing 10 categorical
items per transaction. With minimum support of 0.01 and minimum confidence
of 0.30, the analysis generated 16,304 frequent itemsets and 68,745 rules.
Filtering for rules with a severity consequent retained 3,205 rules.

No qualifying fatal-consequent rules were found at the selected minimum
support threshold. This does not mean that fatal collisions have no
associations; the support requirement can exclude patterns that are
uncommon in the full dataset.

Serious-consequent rules included a maximum confidence of approximately
0.3182 and maximum lift of approximately 1.3994. Slight-consequent rules
included a maximum confidence of approximately 0.9636 and maximum lift
of approximately 1.2713.

Association rules describe co-occurrence under the selected encoding and
thresholds. They do not establish causation or prove that an antecedent
factor caused a collision or its severity. Rules involving special or
unknown category codes require particular caution.

Changes in some categorical-code distributions, especially in recent
years, may reflect changes in reporting specifications or coding practice.
They should be investigated against official documentation before being
interpreted as real changes in road conditions or collision behaviour.

8. SPARK PERFORMANCE BENCHMARK
------------------------------
The benchmark compared Spark aggregation with local Python/pandas
aggregation for a 5 km grid-year aggregation task.

Three measured repetitions produced these runtimes:

Spark: 0.253, 0.239, 0.212 seconds
Local Python/pandas: 0.0314, 0.0263, 0.0296 seconds

Median runtime:
Spark: approximately 0.2391 seconds
Local Python/pandas: approximately 0.0296 seconds

The observed median ratio was approximately 8.07x, with local processing
faster in this particular experiment.

This result does not prove that local processing is generally superior.
The benchmark used one dataset size, in-memory inputs and a local Spark
configuration. The timed work was not perfectly equivalent: Spark derived
grid keys during its timed run, whereas the local method used pre-derived
keys. Results should therefore be presented as a limited benchmark, not a
general scalability conclusion. Larger datasets, repeated trials and
carefully matched operations would be needed for stronger conclusions.

9. MAIN LIMITATIONS
-------------------
1. Collision counts are not adjusted for traffic volume, vehicle kilometres,
   road usage or population exposure.
2. High-count grid cells are screening results rather than statistically
   validated hotspots.
3. Grid resolution and threshold choices influence the spatial pattern.
4. The analysis does not model individual road segments or network effects.
5. Severity classes are imbalanced, and fatal-class predictions have low
   precision in the class-weighted model.
6. Association rules are descriptive and do not establish causation.
7. Changes in coding or reporting may affect comparisons between years.
8. Spatial coverage depends on coordinate availability and boundary matching.
9. The performance benchmark is limited to one dataset and one local setup.
10. Model performance may change when applied to future years or another area.

10. FUTURE IMPROVEMENTS
-----------------------
Future work could include exposure-adjusted collision rates, statistical
hotspot tests, road-network segmentation, spatial-temporal models,
additional validation across years, calibration and threshold analysis
for severity predictions, and external validation in another study area.

The benchmark could be strengthened using larger datasets, matched
operations, additional repetitions and distributed computing resources.
Potential coding changes should be checked against official source
documentation before interpreting temporal patterns.

11. CONCLUSION
-------------
The project demonstrates a reproducible workflow for processing collision
records, creating spatial summaries, screening high-count grid cells,
examining temporal patterns, predicting severity and mining associations.

The results identify areas and patterns worth further investigation, but
they should be interpreted in light of exposure, grid sensitivity,
class imbalance, coding changes and benchmark limitations. The outputs
are suitable for exploratory analysis and research, not as direct proof
of causation or as a replacement for formal road-safety assessment.
